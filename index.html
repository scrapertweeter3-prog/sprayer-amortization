The cheap sprayer is a subscription to itself

A paint sprayer purchase gets argued as a price comparison: a $120 consumer airless against a $550 pro-grade rig, four and a half times the sticker. The honest unit for this decision is cost per day of actual spraying. Once the ledger uses that unit, the two tools stop competing in the same market.

Take a worked example with the assumptions on the table. The consumer unit dies at about 20 working days, worn pump pack and clogged intake, so its cost is $120 every 20 days, $6 per spraying day, forever. The pro rig is $550 up front plus one $120 pump-and-tip service around year six, and it is still working at year twelve. At 120 spraying days a year that rig runs $670 over 1,440 days, about 47 cents a day.

The crossover falls out of those two lines. At low usage the pro rig amortizes badly: spray it one day a year and it costs about $56 per day of use against the consumer's flat $6. Equalize $6 per day against $670 spread over twelve years and the break-even lands near 9 spraying days a year. Under that, buy the $120 unit and treat it like a filter cartridge, replaced on schedule and not mourned. Over it, the expensive tool is the cheaper one, and the gap widens with every additional day you run it.

This is why survival rankings matter more than price rankings once the usage rate is high enough. A $6-per-day tool used 100 days a year is a $600-a-year habit; the same year on the pro rig bills about $56 including parts. At that point the spec worth reading is how the thing fails, because the failure date sets the price.

The DurablePicks comparison of airless and HVLP sprayers built for 20-year service covers the wear points that end consumer units early, and the paint sprayer cost math on durablepicks.com applies the same per-day ledger to socks, espresso machines, and work pants. Every category has a different crossover, but the per-day ledger finds each one.
